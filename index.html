PEMF research

What the published research says about PEMF and shockwave.

Pulsed electromagnetic field therapy and shockwave therapy have each been studied for more than 40 years. This site collects the published studies, checks each one against the original paper, summarizes it in plain English and links to it.

Selected findings

Eight studies worth reading first.

Each one opens on PubMed. The full database has 116, every entry checked against the original paper and summarized in plain English.

  1. 636patients

    PEMF: more pain relief than placebo in osteoarthritis

    Cochrane review of 9 randomized trials: electromagnetic field therapy gave about 15 points more pain relief on a 0–100 scale than placebo, with no serious side effects. Physical function did not clearly improve.

    Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 2013PubMed
  2. 16placebo-controlled trials

    PEMF: pain, stiffness and function in osteoarthritis

    Meta-analysis of placebo-controlled randomized trials only: PEMF beat placebo for osteoarthritis pain, stiffness and physical function. The evidence covers the short term.

    Physical Therapy, 2020PubMed
  3. 1,468fracture patients

    PEMF: fracture healing

    Meta-analysis of 22 randomized trials: PEMF raised the healing rate to 79.7%, against 64.3% with sham treatment, and reduced fracture pain.

    Bioelectromagnetics, 2020PubMed
  4. 144patients

    Shockwave: calcific shoulder tendinitis

    Double-blind trial at the Technical University of Munich: at 6 months, shoulder function scores rose 31.0 points with high-energy shockwave, against 6.6 with sham.

    JAMA, 2003PubMed
  5. 250patients

    Shockwave: chronic heel pain

    Double-blind multicenter trial in chronic plantar fasciitis: at 12 weeks, the median drop in heel pain was 69.2% with focused shockwave, against 34.5% with placebo.

    J Bone Joint Surg Am, 2015PubMed
  6. 336patients

    Shockwave: diabetic foot ulcers

    Two sham-controlled phase III trials: adding shockwave to standard care closed 37.8% of hard-to-heal ulcers by 24 weeks, against 26.2% with sham.

    J Wound Care, 2018PubMed
  7. 54.1%vs 0% with sham

    Shockwave: erectile function when pills fail

    Sham-controlled trial at Sheba Medical Center in 58 men whom ED pills did not help: after low-intensity shockwave, 54.1% reached erections firm enough for intercourse with their pills. A 2025 Cochrane review rates the overall evidence as low certainty.

    J Urol, 2016PubMed
  8. 75%vs 21.7% with sham

    Magnetic stimulation: stress urinary incontinence

    Double-blind trial in 120 women: after 2 months of pulsed magnetic stimulation, 75% responded, against 21.7% with sham, and most responders still responded at 14 months.

    J Urol, 2017PubMed

By condition

What has been studied, condition by condition.

Every study in the database is filed under the condition it looked at. Each card opens the matching studies.

  • Osteoarthritis and joints

    16 studies: 7 systematic reviews, 8 randomized trials, 1 narrative review

    PEMF: a Cochrane review and a meta-analysis of 16 placebo-controlled trials found less pain; gains in function were less clear. Shockwave: a meta-analysis of 14 knee trials found less pain and better function.

    See the 16 studies Knee osteoarthritis brief

  • Back and neck pain

    8 studies: 3 systematic reviews, 3 randomized trials, 1 clinical study, 1 lab study

    A meta-analysis of 14 PEMF trials found more pain relief than placebo in low back pain. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 50 trials compares magnetic stimulation, shockwave and PEMF.

    See the 8 studies Low back pain guide

  • Tendon, heel and soft-tissue pain

    27 studies: 8 systematic reviews, 13 randomized trials, 2 clinical studies, 3 narrative reviews, 1 lab study

    Shockwave beat placebo in a JAMA trial of calcific shoulder tendinitis and a 250-patient heel-pain trial, but other trials in tennis elbow and heel pain found no difference. PEMF trials here are smaller and mixed.

    See the 27 studies Shoulder brief

  • Fibromyalgia

    4 studies: 1 systematic review, 3 randomized trials

    Four studies of PEMF and magnetic stimulation. A 2025 meta-analysis of 6 sham-controlled trials found pain relief in the short term.

    See the 4 studies

  • Fractures, fusion and bone health

    17 studies: 8 systematic reviews, 4 randomized trials, 1 clinical study, 4 narrative reviews

    A meta-analysis of 22 trials found higher fracture healing rates with PEMF, while reviews found no effect on fresh fractures or spinal fusion rates. Shockwave matched surgery in one nonunion trial.

    See the 17 studies

  • Erectile function and Peyronie's

    12 studies: 5 systematic reviews, 7 randomized trials

    Mostly shockwave: five sham-controlled trials in erectile dysfunction (one negative), several meta-analyses, and a 2025 Cochrane review that rates the benefit as small and the evidence as low certainty. In Peyronie's disease, shockwave eased pain but not curvature.

    See the 12 studies

  • Chronic pelvic pain

    5 studies: 2 systematic reviews, 3 randomized trials

    In men with chronic pelvic pain, a Cochrane review rates the short-term benefit of shockwave as high-quality evidence; evidence for magnetic chairs is uncertain. One trial combined the two.

    See the 5 studies

  • Urinary incontinence

    3 studies: 1 systematic review, 2 randomized trials

    A review of 24 trials found magnetic stimulation improved urinary incontinence. Of two sham-controlled trials in women, the larger (120 women) was positive and the other was not.

    See the 3 studies

  • Headache and migraine

    1 randomized trial

    A double-blind trial in 42 migraine sufferers at Madigan Army Medical Center, Tacoma.

    See the study

  • Wounds and recovery after surgery

    9 studies: 3 systematic reviews, 4 randomized trials, 2 narrative reviews

    Shockwave closed more diabetic foot ulcers than sham in two phase III trials. PEMF eased pain after cesarean section and breast reconstruction; Cochrane found no clear benefit for pressure or venous ulcers.

    See the 9 studies

  • Nerves, stroke and spasticity

    8 studies: 4 systematic reviews, 3 randomized trials, 1 narrative review

    Shockwave and magnetic stimulation both reduced spasticity after stroke in meta-analyses. A large PEMF trial in diabetic nerve pain was negative.

    See the 8 studies

    2 randomized trials

    Two Mayo Clinic trials, in high blood pressure and in metabolic syndrome.

Shockwave therapy

What shockwave is, and where the evidence is strongest.

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy sends short, high-pressure sound pulses into tissue from a device held against the skin. It grew out of kidney-stone treatment, first reported in The Lancet in 1980.

Focused devices concentrate the energy at a depth. Radial devices spread it from the skin. Low-intensity shockwave uses lower energy and is studied mainly for erectile dysfunction.

Strongest evidence
Calcific shoulder tendinitis (JAMA, 2003), chronic heel pain (JBJS, 2015) and diabetic foot ulcers (two phase III trials, 2018).
Mixed or negative
Some placebo-controlled trials in tennis elbow and heel pain found no difference, and a Cochrane review judged the gains in rotator cuff disease too small to matter.
FDA, tendons and heel
Premarket approval for chronic plantar fasciitis since 2000 and for tennis elbow since 2002, each for specific devices. Some of those approvals were later withdrawn.
FDA, wounds
De Novo authorization for chronic diabetic foot ulcers in 2017 (DEN160037).
Erectile dysfunction
We found no FDA approval or clearance for this use (FDA databases, October 2026). The American Urological Association (2018) calls it investigational; the European Association of Urology (2026) gives it a weak recommendation for mild vascular cases.

PEMF and shockwave together

Combining the two: an early, honest picture.

Many clinics use the two together. This is what the published research shows so far, and what it does not.

  • Three small randomized trials have tested them together: rotator cuff tendinopathy (86 patients, Kiel), chronic pelvic pain in men (65, Shanghai) and erectile dysfunction (40, Moscow). Each favored the combination. None has been repeated yet.
  • Head to head, results depend on the condition. In a 120-person knee osteoarthritis trial, PEMF helped less than shockwave. In back pain and heel spurs, the two performed similarly.
  • Network meta-analyses rank them differently. Magnetic stimulation came out ahead of shockwave for chronic low back pain and stroke spasticity; shockwave came out ahead for chronic pelvic pain in men.
  • The decisive trials are under way. Two trials testing each therapy alone and together have published their protocols, but no results yet.

Until those trials report, combining the two is best described as a promising approach that is still being studied, not an established treatment.

Since 1979

A short history of PEMF and shockwave research.

  1. 1979

    The first FDA approval

    The FDA grants premarket approval to a PEMF bone-growth stimulator for fractures that fail to heal (PMA P790002). The FDA record, explained

  2. 1980

    Shockwaves enter medicine

    Doctors at Klinikum Grosshadern in Munich report in The Lancet that shockwaves aimed from outside the body break up kidney stones, the start of medical shockwave use. PubMed

  3. 1982

    JAMA reports 1,078 cases

    Columbia-Presbyterian Medical Center reports on 1,007 fractures that had failed to unite and 71 failed fusions: 81% success at Columbia and 79% internationally. PubMed

  4. 1993

    The first osteoarthritis trials

    Double-blind, placebo-controlled trials at Danbury Hospital, Connecticut, in knee and neck osteoarthritis, published in the Journal of Rheumatology in 1993 and 1994. PubMed

  5. 1999

    Migraine, at an Army medical center

    A double-blind trial in 42 migraine sufferers at Madigan Army Medical Center, Tacoma. PubMed

  6. 2000

    Shockwave approved for heel pain

    The FDA grants premarket approval to a shockwave device for chronic plantar fasciitis that has not responded to other care (PMA P990086). An approval for tennis elbow follows in 2002.

  7. 2003

    Shockwave in JAMA

    A double-blind trial at the Technical University of Munich in 144 patients with calcific shoulder tendinitis. PubMed

  8. 2003

    A NASA laboratory study

    NASA's Johnson Space Center publishes a study of human neural cells grown in a time-varying electromagnetic field. What it found

  9. 2008

    Cleared for pain and swelling after surgery

    The FDA clears a PEMF device for postoperative pain and edema through the 510(k) route (K070541). Clearance belongs to that device and indication only.

  10. 2012

    Shockwave for erectile dysfunction

    Rambam Health Care Campus in Haifa publishes a sham-controlled trial of low-intensity shockwave in 67 men. PubMed

  11. 2013

    A Cochrane review

    Nine randomized trials in osteoarthritis, 636 patients: more pain relief than placebo. PubMed

  12. 2017

    Shockwave for diabetic foot ulcers

    The FDA authorizes a shockwave device for chronic diabetic foot ulcers through the De Novo route (DEN160037).

  13. 2018

    The two together, in a randomized trial

    University Hospital Schleswig-Holstein in Kiel adds a high-energy magnetic therapy to shockwave in 86 patients with rotator cuff tendinopathy. PubMed

  14. 2020

    Mayo Clinic, and 22 fracture trials

    Two Mayo Clinic randomized trials, and a meta-analysis of 22 trials in fracture healing (1,468 patients). PubMed

  15. 2025

    A US multi-center trial, and a Cochrane review

    A PEMF trial at Andrews Institute, Northwell and Rothman Orthopaedic Institute sites, and a Cochrane review of low-intensity shockwave for erectile dysfunction (21 trials). PubMed

  16. 2026

    Bone stimulators reclassified

    The FDA moves non-invasive bone-growth stimulators, PEMF devices included, from class III to class II, effective May 18, 2026 (91 FR 20352). Federal Register

NASA and PEMF

What NASA actually studied.

PEMF material often says the technology "comes from NASA". The record is narrower, and worth reading as it is.

In September 2003, Thomas J. Goodwin, PhD, of NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston published a technical paper on human neural progenitor cells grown in a time-varying electromagnetic field.

Study type
A laboratory study on cells, not on patients
Finding
Exposed cells multiplied 2.5 to 4 times faster than unexposed cells, and about 320 genes changed their activity
What it does not show
That any treatment works in people, or anything about a particular device
Source
NASA/TP-2003-212054, NASA Technical Reports Server

How to read it

What the studies show, and where they stop.

A research page that only lists good news is not worth much. These are the limits we keep in mind.

  • Most trials are small. 17 of the 54 randomized trials in the database enrolled fewer than 50 people.
  • The clearest benefit is short term. A 2022 Medical University of Vienna review of 10 systematic reviews describes PEMF as effective in the short term in osteoarthritis.
  • Pain moves more than function. The 2013 Cochrane review found more pain relief than placebo, but no clear difference in physical function.
  • Some trials are industry-funded. The 2025 US multi-center trial was sponsored by the device maker, was not blinded and assigned patients alternately rather than at random.
  • Shockwave has negative trials too. Placebo-controlled trials in tennis elbow (JBJS, 2002) and heel pain (JAMA, 2002; BMJ, 2003) found no difference. They are in the database.
  • Guidelines can disagree. On shockwave for erectile dysfunction, the American Urological Association (2018) calls it investigational; the European Association of Urology (2026) gives it a weak recommendation for mild cases.
  • Combination evidence is early. Three small randomized trials so far, none repeated.
  • Lab studies are labelled. They describe cells, not patients.
  • Studies describe the technology. No summary here describes a particular device or clinic.
