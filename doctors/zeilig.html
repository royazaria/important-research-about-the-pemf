On Treatment with Low-Intensity Pulsed Electromagnetic Fields

Millions of women and men worldwide are searching desperately for relief from the suffering caused by pain of every kind and by restricted joint range of motion, and from the marked decline in functional ability that follows. The suffering is immense, and the financial cost to individuals and to the health system (the economic burden) is enormous.

Degenerative joint changes, inflammation of the musculoskeletal system and injuries are major causes of pain and functional limitation.

Standard Treatment

Standard treatment today includes physical methods (physiotherapy, manual therapy) combined with various technologies (ultrasound, laser and so on), medications given orally or by intra-articular injection, and surgery (joint replacement, for example), with results that do not always satisfy patients. To this must be added the side effects of drug therapy, especially when it is given over long periods.

The use of low-intensity pulsed electromagnetic field therapy (PEMF) has gained momentum in recent decades. It is supported by a rich medical literature and is usually offered to patients as a complementary treatment alongside other treatments, such as physiotherapy.

Published in JAMA

As early as 1982, an article in JAMA pointed to the effectiveness of PEMF in healing hard-to-heal (ununited) fractures (1). In 1984, an article in the prestigious journal The Lancet pointed to the effectiveness of PEMF in people with shoulder pain caused by damage to the rotator cuff muscles (2). Publications on the subject later multiplied. In 2016, an article in Rheumatology showed that PEMF was effective in reducing pain in people with knee osteoarthritis (3). A literature review published in 2020 in the Journal of Pain Research pointed to the treatment's effectiveness in reducing pain caused by musculoskeletal conditions, including fibromyalgia (4). That same year, 2020, another review was published in Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy on the effectiveness of PEMF in people suffering from, among other conditions, back pain (discopathies) and tendinitis (5). An article published a year earlier, in 2019, in Pain and Therapy showed the treatment's effectiveness in reducing low back pain (6).

Finally, in 2020 a comprehensive review in Physical Therapy indicated that PEMF, alone or combined with physiotherapy, has a positive effect on pain, stiffness and physical function in people with pain resulting from degenerative changes in the knees, hands and cervical spine.

The mechanism of action of PEMF, although not fully understood, is based on accelerating the clearance of inflammatory substances and stimulating the growth of cartilage cells (7, 8, 9).

Timeline

  • 1979: FDA approved PEMF therapy for stimulating bone growth
  • 1987: FDA approved for adjunct therapy for treating post-operative edema and pain
  • 2004: PEMF is now used as an adjunct to cervical fusion surgery

Editor's note: these dates refer to specific PEMF devices cleared or approved by the US FDA. They do not refer to the equipment PainFree supplies, which holds no FDA clearance or approval.

In summary: There is no doubt that PEMF technology has a place in rehabilitative treatment for populations suffering from pain and functional impairment in one of the medical conditions mentioned in this review.
